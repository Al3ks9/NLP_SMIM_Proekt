\chapter{Background}\label{ch:background}

\section{Text style transfer}

Text style transfer rewrites a text so that one attribute, its style,
changes while its content stays \citep{jin2022survey}. What counts as style
varies from task to task: sentiment, formality, politeness, or the manner of
a particular author. The last kind is the oldest example in the field:
\citet{xu2012shakespeare} paraphrased modern English into Shakespeare's
English and back, using the parallel modern versions of his plays.

\paragraph{Supervised transfer.} When parallel data exists, pairs of texts
with the same content in two styles, the task is ordinary sequence-to-sequence
learning. The best known dataset of this kind, GYAFC, has about 110,000
informal--formal sentence pairs written by crowd workers
\citep{rao2018gyafc}. Parallel data for an individual author's style
practically never exists: nobody writes the same poem twice in two poets'
voices.

\paragraph{Unsupervised transfer.} Most of the literature therefore works
with non-parallel data, texts in each style that are not translations of
each other. One line of work separates content from style in a latent
representation and decodes the content with a different style
\citep{shen2017crossalign}. Another edits the text directly: it deletes the
words that mark the source style and fills the gaps with words typical of
the target style \citep{li2018delete}. A third treats transfer as
paraphrasing: a paraphrase model first strips the style, and a model trained
on one style's texts then rewrites the neutral paraphrase in that style
\citep{krishna2020strap}. Reinforcement learning was used here as well, with
rewards for the target style and for preserving content
\citep{luo2019dualrl}.

\paragraph{Prompting large models.} Large language models can perform
style transfer with no training at all, given an instruction and a few
examples \citep{reif2022recipe}. Infilling methods combine the editing view
with prompting: the words that carry style are marked or masked, and a
language model chooses their replacements, so that the rest of the text,
and with it the content, is left untouched \citep{pegf}. The second of the
earlier approaches in this thesis (Chapter~\ref{ch:path}) follows this
design.

\paragraph{Poetry and author style.} Poetry adds constraints that prose
does not have: lines, stanzas, metre and rhyme. \citet{tikhonov2018guesswho}
generated poetry in the style of particular authors, in English and in
Russian, and evaluated the results both automatically and with human
readers. Their models generate new poems from an author's
corpus; the task in this thesis is harder in one respect, because the
generated poem must also keep the content of a given source poem written
by someone else.

\section{Stylometry and authorship attribution}

Stylometry measures style with countable features of a text, and its
central application is authorship attribution: deciding who wrote a text
\citep{stamatatos2009survey}. The classic study of the disputed
\emph{Federalist} papers showed that the frequencies of common function
words, which an author uses without thinking about them, identify the
author better than the content does \citep{mosteller1964}. Burrows' Delta
\citep{burrows2002delta}, a distance over standardised frequencies of the
most frequent words, became the standard baseline. Modern attribution
combines many feature types (word and character $n$-grams, part-of-speech
patterns, vocabulary richness, sentence and line length) with a supervised
classifier \citep{stamatatos2009survey}. Vocabulary richness is length
dependent, so a measure that does not grow with the text is needed to
compare short poems with long ones; the moving-average type--token ratio
(MATTR) computes the ratio over a sliding window of fixed size
\citep{covington2010mattr}.

This thesis uses stylometry in two roles. A profile of each author's
features defines what the reward asks for, and an author classifier
measures whether a generated poem reads as that author's. When the same
classifier is both rewarded and used to measure success, a model can learn
to satisfy the classifier without writing like the author, so the
evaluation uses a second classifier that the training never sees
(Chapter~\ref{ch:stylometry}).

\section{Knowledge distillation and low-rank adaptation}

Knowledge distillation trains a small \emph{student} model to imitate a
larger \emph{teacher} \citep{hinton2015distilling}. For text generation the
simplest and most effective form trains the student on the teacher's
outputs, as if they were the reference data (sequence-level distillation,
\citealp{kim2016seqkd}). Large language models have made this a common way
of building training data: a strong model writes examples of a task, and a
smaller model is fine-tuned on them \citep{wang2023selfinstruct}. The
students in this thesis are trained the same way, on poems written by the
teacher.

Fine-tuning all the weights of a model with billions of parameters needs
more memory than a single GPU has. Low-rank adaptation (LoRA,
\citealp{hu2022lora}) freezes the original weights and learns, for chosen
weight matrices, a correction in the form of a product of two small
matrices. The trainable parameters are a fraction of a percent of the
model, and after training the correction can be merged into the weights.

\section{Reinforcement learning for language models}

Supervised fine-tuning teaches a model to imitate examples. Reinforcement
learning instead lets the model generate, scores each output with a
\emph{reward}, and changes the model so that high-reward outputs become more
likely. For language models the reward was first learned from human
preferences between pairs of outputs \citep{christiano2017deep,
ziegler2019finetuning}; this reinforcement learning from human feedback
(RLHF) is how instruction-following assistants are trained
\citep{ouyang2022instructgpt}. The optimisation algorithm in that work is
Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO, \citealp{schulman2017ppo}), which needs,
besides the model being trained, a second network of similar size to
estimate the value of each state.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO, \citealp{shao2024deepseekmath})
removes that second network. For each prompt it samples a group of
outputs, scores them, and uses each output's reward relative to the mean
and standard deviation of its group as the advantage. A penalty on the
divergence from a reference model keeps the trained model close to where
it started. GRPO was introduced for mathematical reasoning and later used
to train reasoning models with rewards that can be checked automatically
\citep{deepseek2025r1}. Here the reward is computed, not learned from
preferences: it is the stylometric profile of the target author together
with a measure of content preservation.

Any reward is only an approximation of what is wanted, and a model that
optimises it hard enough finds ways to score well without doing the task.
This \emph{reward hacking} \citep{skalse2022hacking} grows with the strength
of the optimisation \citep{gao2023overoptimization}, and it is why the
reward and the evaluation in this thesis use different measurements.
Training uses the TRL library \citep{vonwerra2020trl}.

\section{Macedonian NLP}

Macedonian is a South Slavic language written in Cyrillic, with rich verb
morphology, a postposed definite article with three forms, and almost no
noun cases. It has far fewer resources than English or than the larger
Slavic languages. The CLASSLA project provides tagging and lemmatisation
for South Slavic languages, built on the Stanza toolkit
\citep{qi2020stanza, ljubesic2019neural, tercon2023classla}; its
Macedonian models assign each token a lemma, a universal part-of-speech
tag, and a MULTEXT-East morphosyntactic description
\citep{erjavec2012multext}. Large general-purpose models do write
Macedonian, but with deviations in grammar and word agreement
\citep{arizankovska2024chatgpt}, and the small open models used as
students in this thesis were trained on very little of it.
The first open foundation model built specifically for Macedonian, with an
8-billion-parameter model, a corpus of 3.5 billion words, an instruction
dataset and a seven-task benchmark, appeared only in 2025
\citep{krsteski2025macedonian}.
